### 2. [high|law_requires] CORRECTION TO A CIRCULATING ERROR. The widely repeated claim that the Data Protection Board can inquire and levy penalties from 13 November 2026 is FALSE. The twelve-month tranche commences only Consent Manager registration and the narrow power to penalise a Consent Manager for breaching its registration conditions. An HR SaaS is not a Consent Manager, so nothing new bites on this product in November 2026.

**Provision:** G.S.R. 843(E) tranche (b); DPDP Act 2023 s.6(9) and s.27(1)(d); DPDP Rules 2025 rule 4

> s.27(1)(d): "on receipt of an intimation of breach of any condition of registration of a Consent Manager, to inquire into such breach and impose penalty as provided in this Act" ; s.6(9): "Every Consent Manager shall be registered with the Board in such manner and subject to such technical, operational, financial and other conditions as may be prescribed."

NEW THIS ROUND — the prior report did not address this and several secondary sources assert the contrary. I read s.27 in full off the Gazette. The Board's general enforcement powers are s.27(1)(a) (breach intimation under s.8(6)), (b) (data principal complaint against a fiduciary), (c) (complaint against a Consent Manager) and (e) (s.37(2) intermediary reference), together with ss.28-34 which contain the inquiry procedure and s.33 penalties. G.S.R. 843(E) places s.27 EXCEPT clause (d) of sub-section (1), and ss.28-34, in the eighteen-month tranche. Only s.27(1)(d) and s.6(9) commence at twelve months, matched by rule 4 (Consent Manager registration) under rule 1(3). PRD CONSEQUENCE: do not model any November 2026 penalty exposure for this product.

### 3. [high|law_requires] Section 7(i) permits employment-purpose processing without consent. It is a lawful ground, NOT an exemption. NOT YET IN FORCE — s.7 is in the eighteen-month tranche.

**Provision:** DPDP Act 2023 s.7(i) (in force ~13 May 2027)

> "7. A Data Fiduciary may process personal data of a Data Principal for any of following uses, namely:— ... (i) for the purposes of employment or those related to safeguarding the employer from loss or liability, such as prevention of corporate espionage, maintenance of confidentiality of trade secrets, intellectual property, classified information or provision of any service or benefit sought by a Data Principal who is an employee."

Quoted verbatim from my own extraction of the Gazette. Confirmed: the chapeau is permissive ("may process"); s.7 sits inside CHAPTER II headed OBLIGATIONS OF DATA FIDUCIARY (which runs ss.4-10); and s.4(1) frames the two grounds as consent or "certain legitimate uses". The exemptions are in s.17 and employment is not among s.17(1)(a)-(f). This ground/exemption distinction is the single most important thing for the PRD not to get wrong. ONE NUANCE THE PRIOR ROUND MISSED: s.17(1)(a) exempts processing "necessary for enforcing any legal right or claim", which may cover employment litigation and disciplinary defence — a narrow but real exemption route distinct from s.7(i).

### 4. [high|law_requires] s.7(i) removes the consent requirement but not the Data Fiduciary's general obligations. Every sub-section of s.8 applies regardless of lawful ground, because s.8 is drafted without reference to legal basis. NOT YET IN FORCE (s.8 is in the eighteen-month tranche).

**Provision:** DPDP Act 2023 s.8(1)-(11) (in force ~13 May 2027)

> s.8(1): "A Data Fiduciary shall, irrespective of any agreement to the contrary or failure of a Data Principal to carry out the duties provided under this Act, be responsible for complying with the provisions of this Act and the rules made thereunder in respect of any processing undertaken by it or on its behalf by a Data Processor." ; s.8(3): "Where personal data processed by a Data Fiduciary is likely to be— (a) used to make a decision that affects the Data Principal; or (b) disclosed to another Data Fiduciary, the Data Fiduciary processing such personal data shall ensure its completeness, accuracy and consistency."

I read s.8(1) through s.8(11) in full and confirm none is qualified by lawful basis. SURVIVING OBLIGATIONS for an employer relying on s.7(i): s.8(3) completeness/accuracy/consistency where data drives a decision affecting the principal or is disclosed onward (catches payroll and performance decisions); s.8(4) technical and organisational measures; s.8(5) reasonable security safeguards; s.8(6) breach intimation to Board AND each affected principal; s.8(7) erasure, qualified by "unless retention is necessary for compliance with any law for the time being in force"; s.8(9) publish DPO or contact person; s.8(10) effective grievance redressal mechanism. s.13 (grievance redressal) and s.14 (nomination) are likewise drafted without a consent qualifier — I checked both — and so survive s.7(i). The PRD must not imply s.7(i) is a blanket exemption.

### 5. [medium|inference] The rights of access (s.11) and correction/erasure (s.12) are textually tied to processing done on consent or on s.7(a), NOT to s.7(i). On a literal reading an employee has no statutory access or correction right against an employer relying purely on s.7(i). This is a textual reading, not a settled position.

**Provision:** DPDP Act 2023 s.11(1) and s.12(1); corroborated by DPDP Rules 2025 rule 14(2) (all in the eighteen-month tranche)

> s.11(1): "The Data Principal shall have the right to obtain from the Data Fiduciary to whom she has previously given consent, including consent as referred to in clause (a) of section 7 (hereinafter referred to as the said Data Fiduciary), for processing of personal data, upon making to it a request in such manner as may be prescribed,—" ; s.12(1): "A Data Principal shall have the right to correction, completion, updating and erasure of her personal data for the processing of which she has previously given consent, including consent as referred to in clause (a) of section 7, in accordance with any requirement or procedure under any law for the time being in force." ; rule 14(2): "To exercise the rights of the Data Principal under the Act, she may make a request to the Data Fiduciary to whom she has previously given consent for processing of her personal data, using the means and furnishing the particulars required by such Data Fiduciary for the exercise of such rights."

CORRECTLY LABELLED AS INFERENCE — this is a reading of the words, not a holding. Both sections name only consent and s.7(a) (voluntary provision); s.7(i) is conspicuously absent. NEW CORROBORATION I ADDED THIS ROUND: rule 14(2) of the Rules repeats the identical qualifier, so the drafting is consistent across Act and Rules rather than a one-off slip in s.11. That raises confidence in the textual reading; it does not resolve the legal question. COUNTER-TEXT: s.12(3), the erasure mechanic, is drafted without repeating the consent qualifier — "A Data Principal shall make a request in such manner as may be prescribed to the Data Fiduciary for erasure of her personal data" — which is arguable support for a wider reading of erasure at least. PRACTICAL COUNTERWEIGHT that stops this mattering much: an employer will rarely rely on s.7(i) alone. Bank details, PAN and address are typically volunteered, engaging s.7(a), which IS named. So rights attach to much of the HR record by a different door. PRODUCT CONCLUSION UNCHANGED: build the rights machinery regardless. FLAGGED FOR COUNSEL.

### 6. [medium|inference] There is no statutory notice obligation attached to pure s.7(i) processing. Section 5 and rule 3 both tie notice to a consent request. Contrary practitioner commentary asserts otherwise without statutory support.

**Provision:** DPDP Act 2023 s.5(1); DPDP Rules 2025 rule 3 (both in the eighteen-month tranche)

> s.5(1): "Every request made to a Data Principal under section 6 for consent shall be accompanied or preceded by a notice given by the Data Fiduciary to the Data Principal, informing her,— (i) the personal data and the purpose for which the same is proposed to be processed; (ii) the manner in which she may exercise her rights under sub-section (4) of section 6 and section 13; and (iii) the manner in which the Data Principal may make a complaint to the Board, in such manner and as may be prescribed." ; rule 3(b): notice shall "give, in clear and plain language, a fair account of the details necessary to enable the Data Principal to give specific and informed consent for the processing of her personal data"

I read s.5 and rule 3 in full. s.5(1) is triggered by a consent request under s.6. Rule 3 is headed "Notice given by Data Fiduciary to Data Principal" and every limb is consent-framed: (b) enabling "specific and informed consent"; (c)(i) the means to "withdraw her consent, with the ease of doing so being comparable to that with which such consent was given". Neither attaches to s.7(i). WHAT IS REQUIRED REGARDLESS OF BASIS: s.8(9) with rule 9 — publish contact information of the DPO or a person who can answer questions about processing. So a contact point is owed even where a formal notice is not. RECOMMENDATION STANDS: ship an employee privacy notice anyway. Near-zero cost, hedges a real interpretive risk, and every enterprise buyer asks for it in security review. FLAGGED FOR COUNSEL.

### 8. [high|law_requires] Rule 6 specifies minimum security safeguards with unusual concreteness — encryption or masking, access control, logging with detection capability, backups, and a one-year log retention floor. Most build-relevant provision in the framework. NOT YET IN FORCE (eighteen-month tranche).

**Provision:** DPDP Rules 2025 rule 6(1)(a)-(g) (in force ~13 May 2027)

> chapeau: "A Data Fiduciary shall protect personal data in its possession or under its control, including in respect of any processing undertaken by it or on its behalf by a Data Processor, by taking reasonable security safeguards to prevent personal data breach, which shall include, at the minimum, — (a) appropriate data security measures, such as securing of personal data through encryption, obfuscation, masking or the use of virtual tokens mapped to that personal data; (b) appropriate measures to control access to the computer resources used by such Data Fiduciary or such a Data Processor, wherever applicable; (c) visibility on the accessing of such personal data, through appropriate logs, monitoring and review, for enabling detection of unauthorised access, its investigation and remediation to prevent recurrence; (d) reasonable measures for continued processing in the event of confidentiality, integrity or availability of such personal data being compromised as a result of destruction or loss of access to personal data or otherwise, such as by way of data-backups; (e) for enabling the detection of unauthorised access, its investigation, remediation to prevent recurrence and continued processing in the event of such a compromise, retain such logs and personal data for a period of one year, unless compliance with any law for the time being in force requires otherwise; (f) appropriate provision in the contract entered into between such Data Fiduciary and such a Data Processor, wherever applicable, for taking reasonable security safeguards; and (g) appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure effective observance of security safeguards."

Verified verbatim, and I have restored the purposive opening of clause (e) which the prior round elided behind an ellipsis — the retention duty is expressly tied to detection, investigation, remediation and continuity, which matters if you ever argue about what must be logged. The chapeau extends the duty to processing done "on its behalf by a Data Processor", so the employer's obligation reaches into the vendor's stack. Clause (a) is drafted as "such as": encryption is illustrative, not mandated in terms, and obfuscation, masking or virtual tokens would satisfy it. Clause (c) requires detection capability, not merely the existence of logs. Penalty exposure on the parent obligation (s.8(5)) is the largest head in the Schedule at Rs 250 crore.

### 9. [high|law_requires] Breach notification has no materiality threshold. Every personal data breach is notifiable, to both the Board and every affected data principal, on two different clocks. The definition is very wide and includes accidental loss of access. NOT YET IN FORCE (rule 7 and s.8(6) both eighteen-month tranche).

**Provision:** DPDP Rules 2025 rule 7(1) and 7(2); DPDP Act s.8(6) and s.2(u) (s.2(u) in force; the obligations are not)

> s.2(u): "'personal data breach' means any unauthorised processing of personal data or accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction or loss of access to personal data, that compromises the confidentiality, integrity or availability of personal data" ; rule 7(1): "On becoming aware of any personal data breach, the Data Fiduciary shall, to the best of its knowledge, intimate to each affected Data Principal, in a concise, clear and plain manner and without delay, through her user account or any mode of communication registered by her with the Data Fiduciary, —" ; rule 7(2)(b): "within seventy-two hours of becoming aware of the breach, or within such longer period as the Board may allow on a request made in writing in this behalf, —"

Verified verbatim, both sub-rules and all specified content items. TO EACH AFFECTED DATA PRINCIPAL (r.7(1)), without delay, five items: (a) description including nature, extent and timing; (b) consequences relevant to her; (c) mitigation measures implemented and being implemented; (d) safety measures she may take; (e) business contact information of a person who can respond. TO THE BOARD (r.7(2)): (a) without delay, description including nature, extent, timing and location and likely impact; then (b) within 72 hours, six items including updated detail, broad facts and reasons, mitigation, findings on who caused it, remedial measures, and "a report regarding the intimations given to affected Data Principals". CONTRAST WITH GDPR: no risk-based carve-out, no "unlikely to result in a risk" filter, no de minimis. A single misdirected payslip is textually notifiable to that employee.

### 10. [high|law_requires] The 3-year inactivity erasure rule does NOT apply to an HR SaaS or its employer customers. The Third Schedule covers only large e-commerce, large online gaming and large social media intermediaries.

**Provision:** DPDP Rules 2025 rule 8(1)-(2) with Third Schedule (eighteen-month tranche)

> Third Schedule, column (2) entries: "Data Fiduciary who is an e-commerce entity having not less than two crore registered users in India"; "Data Fiduciary who is an online gaming intermediary having not less than fifty lakh registered users in India"; "Data Fiduciary who is a social media intermediary having not less than two crore registered users in India" — each with "Three years from the date on which the Data Principal last approached the Data Fiduciary for the performance of the specified purpose or exercise of her rights, or the commencement of the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025, whichever is latest."

I read the full Third Schedule including its Note, which defines e-commerce entity by reference to the Consumer Protection Act 2019, online gaming intermediary, social media intermediary by reference to rule 2(1)(w) of the IT Intermediary Guidelines 2021, and "user". An HR/payroll SaaS is none of the three, and neither is a 20-200 person employer. CONSEQUENCE: the 3-year auto-erasure and the 48-hour pre-erasure warning in rule 8(2) are NOT obligations for this product. Vendor marketing presenting the 3-year rule as generally applicable is wrong. The general erasure duty in s.8(7) still applies but is untimed and yields to retention required by law.

Source: https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf

### 11. [high|law_requires] Rule 8(3) imposes a universal one-year MINIMUM retention of personal data and processing logs on every Data Fiduciary, and requires the fiduciary to make its processor do the same. It cuts against the erasure right. NOT YET IN FORCE (eighteen-month tranche).

**Provision:** DPDP Rules 2025 rule 8(3) with Seventh Schedule (in force ~13 May 2027)

> "Without prejudice to sub-rules (1) and (2), a Data Fiduciary shall retain, in respect of any processing of personal data undertaken by it or on its behalf by a Data Processor, such personal data, associated traffic data and other logs of the processing for a minimum period of one year from the date of such processing, for the purposes as specified in the Seventh Schedule, after which the Data Fiduciary shall cause such personal data and logs to be erased, unless further retention is required for compliance with any other law for the time being in force or notified by the Government."

Verified verbatim, and the Illustration Case 2 is verbatim too and directly on point for a SaaS architecture: "X, a company engages a cloud service provider C as its Data Processor to host customer records. - Do NOT build the 3-year inactivity auto-erasure with 48-hour advance warning. The Third Schedule applies only to large e-commerce, online gaming and social media intermediaries. Neither this product nor a 20-200 person employer is in scope. Building it would delete live HR records the employer is statutorily required to keep.
  - priority: P1 | driven_by: DPDP Rules r.8(1)-(2) and Third Schedule (inapplicable classes)
- Cross-border: a US-hosted AI provider is permissible under DPDP as currently drafted — no transfer mechanism, no SCCs, no consent needed. But architect for a future localisation switch: keep data residency configurable per tenant and keep the AI inference path swappable to an India-hosted model. Rule 13(4) permits localisation orders for SDFs, and MeitY has signalled intent to notify SDF cross-border restrictions early.
  - priority: P1 | driven_by: DPDP Act s.16(1); DPDP Rules r.15, r.13(4)
- Pursue ISO/IEC 27001 certification on the current timeline. Until s.44(2) commences (~May 2027) the SPDI Rules 2011 remain in force and rule 8 names IS/ISO/IEC 27001 as the reasonable-security-practices standard for sensitive personal data. After that DPDP names no standard, but the certification remains the de facto buyer requirement.
  - priority: P1 | driven_by: IT Act s.43A and SPDI Rules 2011 rule 8 (in force until s.44(2) commences); commercial necessity thereafter
- Provide a nomination feature letting an employee nominate one or more individuals to exercise their rights on death or incapacity, with the employer able to define required particulars under its terms of service. Rarely built by competitors and cheap to add; s.14 is not qualified by lawful basis so it survives s.7(i).
  - priority: P2 | driven_by: DPDP Act s.14(1)-(2); DPDP Rules r.14(4)
- Do NOT build SDF features (annual DPIA, independent audit, India-based DPO answerable to the board, algorithmic due diligence) into v1. Designation is discretionary, requires an affirmative government notification, none has been found, and neither the vendor nor a 20-200 employee customer is a plausible candidate. Track as a roadmap contingency only.
  - priority: P2 | driven_by: DPDP Act s.10(1)-(2); DPDP Rules r.13(1)-(5)

## Needs counsel

- THE BIGGEST ONE: whether the rights of access (s.11) and correction/erasure (s.12) apply at all to employee data processed under s.7(i). Both sections are textually limited to processing 'for which she has previously given consent, including consent as referred to in clause (a) of section 7', and rule 14(2) of the Rules repeats the same qualifier — so the drafting is consistent across both instruments, not a slip. Against that, s.12(3) states the erasure mechanic without repeating the qualifier. I read the text as excluding pure s.7(i) processing but I checked practitioner literature specifically for this point and found it simply not addressed. If counsel confirms the rights do not attach, the compliance story changes materially; if counsel says they do attach (e.g. by reading s.7 uses as a species of deemed consent), the rights machinery becomes a legal obligation rather than a hedge. Do not resolve this from desk research.
- Whether an employer relying on s.7(i) owes any notice obligation. s.5(1) is triggered by a consent request under s.6, and every limb of rule 3 is framed around enabling informed consent and enabling its withdrawal — so on the text no notice is owed for pure s.7(i) processing. Yet practitioner commentary routinely asserts employees must receive a privacy notice. Counsel should say whether that is a legal requirement, a reading-in from s.8's transparency architecture, or best practice described loosely.
- The precise outer boundary of 'for the purposes of employment' in s.7(i). Recruitment of candidates who are not yet employees, background verification, continuous biometric attendance capture, productivity or location monitoring, and retention of ex-employee records all sit at the edge. The Rules impose no conditions on this legitimate use, which makes the boundary purely a matter of construction. Biometric attendance deserves a specific opinion — whether capturing and retaining a face template is 'for the purposes of employment' or requires an independent basis. Counsel should also address whether s.17(1)(a) ('necessary for enforcing any legal right or claim') provides a distinct route for disciplinary and litigation-hold processing.
- Whether a Data Processor can be penalised directly by the Board. s.33(1) permits penalty where 'a person' commits a significant breach and Schedule entry 7 covers 'any other provision', but all six references to 'Data Processor' in the Rules frame it as the object of the fiduciary's duty, every substantive duty in s.8 is drafted onto the Data Fiduciary, and s.8(1) makes the fiduciary responsible 'irrespective of any agreement to the contrary'. Whether an HR SaaS vendor carries independent statutory exposure or only contractual exposure to its customer is unresolved on the text and determines the vendor's own risk posture and insurance.
- How to reconcile the rule 8(3) one-year minimum retention with an employee's s.12(3) erasure request, and whether soft-delete with suppression discharges the obligation to 'erase'. s.12(3) yields to retention 'necessary for compliance with any law', and rule 8(3) is such a law, but whether retaining suppressed data satisfies 'erase' is a construction question with direct architectural consequences.
- Which statutory retention periods under Indian labour, tax and social-security law override DPDP erasure for payroll records, statutory registers, PF/ESI records and Form 16 data, and for how long after employment ends. This is the practical content of the 'unless retention is necessary for compliance with any law' carve-out and cannot be answered without a labour-law review. It should produce a concrete retention schedule the product can implement.
- Whether the SPDI Rules 2011 written-consent requirement in rule 5(1) ('consent in writing through letter or Fax or email ... before collection') applies to employee biometric attendance capture and to collection of bank account details in the interim period before s.44(2) commences, and whether employment-context consent is regarded as freely given. The interim regime appears STRICTER for this product's payload than DPDP will be, which is counterintuitive and worth an explicit opinion. Note this is now a verified textual position, not a hypothesis.
- Whether the product's employer customers, or the vendor itself, could plausibly be designated Significant Data Fiduciaries as the designation criteria develop — and specifically whether aggregate processing across many employer tenants could be attributed to the vendor for the 'volume and sensitivity' factor in s.10(1)(a). Speculative, but it determines whether rule 13(4) localisation is a live architectural risk.
- The Aadhaar Act 2016 and UIDAI regulations as they apply to storing Aadhaar numbers and biometric templates in an HR system. A separate statutory regime that DPDP does not displace, carrying criminal as well as civil exposure, and expressly outside the scope of this research dimension. It is the largest remaining unfilled legal gap in the PRD and needs both counsel and its own research pass.
- Whether the s.39 bar on civil court jurisdiction — already in force since 13 November 2025, even though the Board's substantive powers are not — creates any interim gap in remedies that affects contractual risk allocation with customers. Low priority, but it is an odd commencement artefact worth a sentence from counsel.

- Has any amendment to the DPDP Act or Rules been gazetted between November 2025 and September 2026? I found none and targeted searches returned nothing, but I could not prove the negative from a primary source because meity.gov.in and indiacode.nic.in both return HTTP 403 to programmatic fetch and the MeitY site is a client-rendered SPA. - Does the proposed compression from 18 to 12 months apply to all data fiduciaries or only to Significant Data Fiduciaries? - What is the exact eighteen-month commencement date — 13 or 14 May 2027? Both G.S.R. 843(E) and G.S.R. 846(E) are dated 13 November 2025, both carry a CG-DL-E stamp of 14 November 2025, and both count from 'the date of publication of this gazette'. ## Retracted

- RETRACTED FROM CIRCULATION (not from the prior round's own work, which had already caught it — I independently corroborated the catch): the claim that 'Digital Personal Data Protection (Amendment) Rules, 2026' was notified vide G.S.R. 120(E) dated 10 February 2026. I read the MeitY PDF: the 10 February 2026 instrument and its accompanying FAQ are the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2026 — an entirely different instrument, with zero occurrences of 'digital personal data' in the FAQ text. There is no DPDP Amendment Rules 2026 that I could locate. This false citation is circulating and must not enter the PRD.
- NEWLY RETRACTED THIS ROUND: the widely circulating claim that from 13 November 2026 the Data Protection Board can inquire into breaches and levy penalties. FALSE. G.S.R. 843(E) tranche (b) commences only s.6(9) (Consent Manager registration) and s.27(1)(d), which is confined to 'breach of any condition of registration of a Consent Manager'. The Board's general enforcement powers — s.27(1)(a), (b), (c) and (e) together with ss.28-34, which contain the inquiry procedure and s.33 penalties — sit in the eighteen-month tranche. An HR SaaS is not a Consent Manager. Do not model any November 2026 penalty exposure.
- RETRACTED FROM THE PRIOR ROUND: its claim to have enumerated the 'complete set' of time periods in the Rules, listing seven and asserting 'Nothing else.' The enumeration was incomplete — it missed twelve months (r.13(1) SDF DPIA and audit cycle), six months extendable by three months at a time (r.19(9) Board inquiry completion), seven years (Fourth Schedule para 4(c) Consent Manager record retention) and Board member service periods. The substantive claim the enumeration supported — that r.14(3)'s 90 days is the only data-principal-facing response timeline and that no timeline is prescribed for access, correction, completion, updating or erasure — SURVIVES my own independent enumeration. Only the completeness assertion is withdrawn.
- CORRECTED FROM THE PRIOR ROUND: its statement that 'Data Processor' appears four times in the English text of the Rules. It appears six times — two occurrences wrap across line breaks and one sits in the Second Schedule. The conclusion drawn from the count is unaffected: all six frame the processor as the object of the fiduciary's duty and none imposes a direct duty on the processor.
- CORRECTED FROM THE PRIOR ROUND: its characterisation of the January 2026 MeitY proposal as compressing overall compliance from 18 to 12 months, labelled 'common_practice'. The scope is unresolved in the source material itself, which says it remains to be seen whether the provisions would be enforced for data fiduciaries across the board or only for SDFs; other reporting frames it as SDF-only. The status label is also wrong — a non-gazetted draft proposal is neither law nor common practice.
- CORRECTED FROM THE PRIOR ROUND (systematic, affecting roughly fifteen of its twenty-two findings): obligations stated in the present tense without noting that the provision is not yet in force. Cross-checked against G.S.R. 843(E) and rule 1(2)-(4), Act ss.3-17, 27 (except 27(1)(d)), 28-34, 36, 37 and 44(2), and Rules 3, 5-16, 22 and 23, all commence only at the eighteen-month mark, ~13 May 2027. As of 4 September 2026 the only DPDP provisions in force are s.1(2), s.2, ss.18-26, 35, 38-43, s.44(1) and (3) and Rules 1, 2, 17-21. Every finding above now carries its in-force status.
- STANDS CORRECTED AND CONFIRMED, NOT RETRACTED: the prior round's correction of the widely repeated assertion that the DPDP Rules impose a three-year retention or erasure limit on data fiduciaries generally. Verified against the full Third Schedule and its Note: rule 8(1) and the Third Schedule reach only e-commerce entities with not less than two crore registered users, online gaming intermediaries with not less than fifty lakh registered users, and social media intermediaries with not less than two crore registered users. An HR SaaS and its SMB employer customers are none of these. The general assertion remains wrong and must not enter the PRD.
