### 3. [high|law_requires] THE SPDI RULES 2011 REMAIN IN FORCE TODAY and they DO classify biometric information as sensitive personal data. In September 2026 biometric attendance data IS legally 'sensitive' in India. This flips only on 13 May 2027. The full statutory chain is now verified from primary text end to end.

**Provision:** IT (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules 2011, G.S.R. 313(E) dated 11 April 2011, rules 2(1)(b) and 3(vi); IT Act 2000 s.43A and s.87(2)(ob); DPDP s.44(2)(a) and (c) not yet commenced

> SPDI r.3: "Sensitive personal data or information of a person means such personal information which consists of information relating to;- ... (vi) Biometric information". r.2(1)(b): "'Biometrics' means the technologies that measure and analyse human body characteristics, such as 'fingerprints', 'eye retinas and irises', 'voice patterns', \"facial patterns', 'hand measurements' and 'DNA' for authentication purposes". IT Act s.87(2)(ob): "the reasonable security practices and procedures and sensitive personal data or information under section 43A". IT Act s.43A Explanation (iii): "'sensitive personal data or information' means such personal information as may be prescribed by the Central Government in consultation with such professional bodies or associations as it may deem fit".

CHAIN NOW FULLY PRIMARY. The prior report could not reach IT Act s.43A and reasoned indirectly; I retrieved the consolidated IT Act 2000 from indiacode.nic.in (the site 403s WebFetch but serves the PDF to curl with a browser User-Agent and a Referer header) and read s.43A and s.87(2)(ob) directly. Both are on the statute book, both inserted by Act 10 of 2009 (ss.21 and 46, w.e.f. 27-10-2009), neither shown as omitted. Chain: (1) SPDI preamble - "In exercise of the powers conferred by clause (ob) of sub-section (2) of section 87 read with section 43A"; (2) DPDP s.44(2)(a) omits s.43A and s.44(2)(c) omits s.87(2)(ob); (3) G.S.R. 843(E) para (c) places s.44(2) in the 18-month bucket. Therefore both parent provisions and the Rules made under them stand today. The SPDI definition covers 'facial patterns' and 'fingerprints' expressly - exactly what an attendance terminal captures.

### 4. [medium|law_requires] Today's binding requirement is WRITTEN consent before collecting biometrics, plus a retention limit, a review right and a Grievance Officer answering within one month - all under the SPDI Rules. But the rule 5(7) 'opt-out' is materially weaker than it looks, and a 2011 MeitY clarification may take a contracted SaaS processor outside rules 5 and 6 altogether.

**Provision:** SPDI Rules 2011 rules 5(1), 5(2), 5(3), 5(4), 5(6), 5(7), 5(9); MeitY Press Note dated 24 August 2011 (secondary only)

> r.5(1): "Body corporate or any person on its behalf shall obtain consent in writing through letter or Fax or email from the provider of the sensitive personal data or information regarding purpose of usage before collection of such information." r.5(4): "...shall not retain that information for longer than is required for the purposes for which the information may lawfully be used or is otherwise required under any other law for the time being in force." r.5(7): "...shall, prior to the collection of information including sensitive personal data or information, provide an option to the provider of the information to not to provide the data or information sought to be collected. The provider of information shall, at any time while availing the services or otherwise, also have an option to withdraw its consent given earlier to the body corporate. Such withdrawal of the consent shall be sent in writing to the body corporate. In the case of provider of information not providing or later on withdrawing his consent, the body corporate shall have the option not to provide goods or services for which the said information was sought." r.5(9): Grievance Officer shall redress "expeditiously but within one month from the date of receipt of grievance".

All quoted verbatim from the G.S.R. 313(E) gazette text (signature block '[F. No. 11(3)/2011-CLFE] N. RAVI SHANKER, Jt. Secy.'). TWO CORRECTIONS to the prior report. The full sub-rule ends by expressly preserving the body corporate's "option not to provide goods or services for which the said information was sought". Shipping an alternative remains the right product answer, but on risk-mitigation and proportionality grounds, not because r.5(7) compels it. (2) A MeitY press note of 24 August 2011 is consistently reported as clarifying that a body corporate handling SPDI "under contractual obligation with any legal entity" is not subject to rules 5 and 6, while one contracting directly with the provider of information is - and that consent may be given by any mode of electronic communication. I could not retrieve the press note itself (PIB relid 74990 returns 403), so treat this as secondary. If correct, r.5(1) written consent binds the EMPLOYER, and an HRMS SaaS acting under contract with the employer may sit outside rules 5-6 while remaining fully within rules 7 and 8.

### 5. [medium|inference] From 13 May 2027, s.7(i) 'purposes of employment' is available as a non-consent legal basis. Whether it stretches to BIOMETRIC attendance specifically (as opposed to attendance generally) is untested and is the single biggest legal risk in this dimension.

**Provision:** DPDP Act 2023 s.7(i) (not in force until 13 May 2027)

> s.7: "A Data Fiduciary may process personal data of a Data Principal for any of following uses, namely:— ... (i) for the purposes of employment or those related to safeguarding the employer from loss or liability, such as prevention of corporate espionage, maintenance of confidentiality of trade secrets, intellectual property, classified information or provision of any service or benefit sought by a Data Principal who is an employee."

Quoted verbatim; I read s.7 in full from the gazette text (the unusual phrasing 'for any of following uses' is the Act's own). LAW: the text is broad, carries no necessity or proportionality qualifier on its face, and contains no biometric carve-out (consistent with there being no sensitive category at all). INFERENCE (not law): attendance is naturally 'for the purposes of employment', so s.7(i) probably covers the PURPOSE. But s.7(i) is silent on MEANS, and a Puttaswamy proportionality argument against choosing the most intrusive available means is open. There is no Indian case law, no Data Protection Board decision and no MeitY guidance on this point. Do NOT state in the PRD that s.7(i) authorises biometric capture. Note also that s.6(10)'s burden-of-proof provision applies only "Where a consent given by the Data Principal is the basis of processing" - it does not bite where the employer relies on s.7(i).

Source: https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf

### 6. [high|inference] There is NO Indian authority holding that an employer may impose biometric attendance on an unwilling employee. The October 2025 Supreme Court order widely reported as upholding mandatory biometric attendance in fact turned on the employees NOT opposing it, and decided nothing about privacy or data protection.

**Provision:** Union of India & Ors. v. Dillip Kumar Rout & Ors., Civil Appeal No. 13572/2015, Supreme Court of India, order dated 29 October 2025 (Pankaj Mithal and Prasanna B. Varale JJ)

> Para 6: "Learned counsel for the respondents accepts that the employees are not opposed to the introduction of the Biometric Attendance System." Para 7: "Once the employees have no reservation on the introduction of the Biometric Attendance System, we are of the opinion that no controversy in this regard survives and the department can very well go ahead with the implementation of the above system." Para 9: "Therefore, in the facts and circumstances of the case, when the introduction of the Biometric Attendance System is for the benefit of all the stakeholders, merely for the reason that the employees were not consulted before implementing the same does not render the introduction of the system to be illegal."

RE-VERIFIED against the full order text. Case title, court, appeal number, bench and date all confirmed. Paragraphs 6, 7 and 9 confirmed word-for-word. Confirmed the order contains NO privacy, Article 21, proportionality or data-protection analysis, and that the parties were a public employer (Union of India / Office of the Principal Accountant General) and central government employees under service law - not private employment. The ratio is narrow: absence of prior consultation does not by itself invalidate. Secondary reporting headlining this as 'biometric attendance legal without consultation' overstates it. The claim is labelled 'inference' because it is a negative proposition about the state of Indian authority, not a proposition the order itself states.

Source: https://indiankanoon.org/doc/170713357/

### 7. [high|law_requires] Aadhaar biometrics and private attendance-device biometrics are governed by separate regimes and must never be conflated. A face or fingerprint template captured by an office terminal is NOT Aadhaar data and Aadhaar Act s.29 does not apply to it.

**Provision:** Aadhaar Act 2016 ss.2(g), 2(j), 8(4), 29(1), 30

> s.2(j): "'core biometric information' means finger print, Iris scan, or such other biological attribute of an individual as may be specified by regulations". s.2(g): "'biometric information' means photograph, finger print, Iris scan, or such other biological attributes of an individual as may be specified by regulations". s.29(1): "No core biometric information, collected or created under this Act, shall be— (a) shared with anyone for any reason whatsoever; or (b) used for any purpose other than generation of Aadhaar numbers and authentication under this Act." s.8(4): "The Authority shall respond to an authentication query with a positive, negative or any other appropriate response sharing such identity information excluding any core biometric information." s.30: biometric information collected and stored under the Act "shall be deemed to be 'electronic record' and 'sensitive personal data or information'", with the Explanation defining SPDI "as assigned to it in clause (iii) of the Explanation to section 43A of the Information Technology Act, 2000".

All verified verbatim from the UIDAI-published consolidated Act. ONE CORRECTION to the prior report: it said "by s.8(4) a requesting entity NEVER receives Aadhaar biometric templates, only a yes/no". The first half is right, the second overstates s.8(4), which permits "any other appropriate response sharing such identity information excluding any core biometric information" - so an authentication or e-KYC response may return demographic identity information and, notably, a PHOTOGRAPH, because s.2(g) makes photograph 'biometric information' while s.2(j) deliberately excludes it from 'core biometric information'. The accurate statement is: a requesting entity never receives Aadhaar fingerprint or iris templates, but may receive other identity information including a photograph. The rest holds: s.29 restrictions bite only on information 'collected or created under this Act', so a private device's templates fall outside it entirely.

Source: https://uidai.gov.in/images/Aadhaar_Act_2016_as_amended.pdf

**Provision:** n/a - vendor technical documentation

### 12. [high|inference] Biometric templates are NOT reliably irreversible. Peer-reviewed survey work establishes that a synthetic sample matching the original can be reconstructed from an unprotected template. Templates must be treated as personal data, never as anonymised hashes.

**Provision:** n/a - peer-reviewed technical literature; relevant to DPDP s.2(t) 'identifiable by or in relation to such data'

> Gomez-Barrero & Galbally: "As a result, the early common belief among the biometrics community of templates irreversibility has been proven wrong."

CITATION AND QUOTE BOTH VERIFIED. Marta Gomez-Barrero and Javier Galbally, 'Reversing the Irreversible: A Survey on Inverse Biometrics', Elsevier Computers & Security, Vol. 90, March 2020. I checked the arXiv identifier the prior report gave (2401.02861) because a 2024-series ID on a 2020 paper looked wrong - it is legitimate: the authors posted the published paper to arXiv in 2024, and the abstract at that ID contains the quoted sentence word for word. INFERENCE: because a template can be inverted to a sample that identifies the individual, a template falls within DPDP s.2(t). Reject any 'we only store a hash, so it isn't personal data' claim. ISO/IEC 24745 template protection (irreversibility, unlinkability, revocability) is the mitigating control; commodity attendance devices generally do not implement it.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02861

## Retracted

- 'The DPDP Act classifies biometric data as sensitive personal data' — FALSE. Independently re-verified: zero occurrences of 'biometric', 'sensitive' or 'special categor' in the full text of both the Act (1064 lines) and the Rules (946 lines). This claim is widespread in vendor and HR-blog marketing (I encountered it again during this check, on an HR-software blog dated 2026) and should be removed wherever it appears.
- 'The SPDI Rules 2011 have been repealed / are no longer in force' — FALSE as of September 2026. Now settled from primary text on both sides: IT Act s.43A and s.87(2)(ob) are live on the statute book at indiacode.nic.in, and their omission sits in DPDP s.44(2), which G.S.R. 843(E) places in the 18-month bucket commencing 13 May 2027.
- 'The DPDP Act is now in force and its obligations apply' — MISLEADING. Only s.1(2), s.2, ss.18-26, s.35, ss.38-43 and s.44(1) and (3) commenced on 13 November 2025. Every substantive obligation and right (ss.3-17) and every penalty provision (ss.28-34) commences 13 May 2027. DPDP Rules 3 and 5 to 16 likewise (r.1(4)).
- 'The Supreme Court has upheld mandatory biometric attendance for employees' — OVERSTATED. Union of India v. Dillip Kumar Rout (29 October 2025) was disposed of on the express footing that the employees did not oppose the system, concerned central government employees under service law, and contains no privacy or data-protection analysis. Re-verified paragraph by paragraph.
- 'DPDP requires biometric data to be stored in India / imposes data localisation' — FALSE. s.16(1) is an unexercised negative-list power and no restricted-country notification has issued. Rule 15 is narrower still: on its face it concerns making personal data available to a foreign State or its agencies, and no order has been made under it. The genuine India-residency pressures come from SPDI rule 7 and the CERT-In 180-day log direction.
- 'A biometric template is a one-way hash and therefore not personal data' — FALSE as a technical matter. Gomez-Barrero & Galbally, Computers & Security vol. 90 (2020): quote verified verbatim in the paper's abstract.
- 'The DPDP Rules require erasure of inactive personal data after three years' — INAPPLICABLE here. That obligation flows from r.8(1) and the Third Schedule, which reach only e-commerce entities with 2 crore+ users, online gaming intermediaries with 50 lakh+, and social media intermediaries with 2 crore+.
- NEW RETRACTION — 'SPDI rule 5(7) gives employees a right to opt out of biometric collection' — OVERSTATED, and the prior report quoted only the first sentence of the sub-rule. The full text ends: 'In the case of provider of information not providing or later on withdrawing his consent, the body corporate shall have the option not to provide goods or services for which the said information was sought.' Rule 5(7) confers a right to refuse the data, not a right to an alternative service. Shipping a non-biometric alternative remains correct product design, but the PRD must not present it as compelled by r.5(7).
- NEW RETRACTION — 'Under Aadhaar Act s.8(4) a requesting entity receives only a yes/no' — OVERSTATED. s.8(4) permits 'a positive, negative or any other appropriate response sharing such identity information excluding any core biometric information'. Because s.2(g) makes photograph 'biometric information' while s.2(j) excludes it from 'core biometric information', an authentication or e-KYC response may return identity information including a photograph. The accurate statement is that a requesting entity never receives Aadhaar fingerprint or iris templates.
- NEW RETRACTION — 'A data protection board decision in 2026 held biometric attendance unlawful even with consent' — TRUE, BUT NOT INDIAN. That is the Turkish KVKK's Principle Decision No. 2026/921 (29 April 2026, published 2 June 2026) under Law No. 6698. It has no application in India. Aggregator republication without a country in the headline makes this an easy and serious mis-citation.
- NEW RETRACTION — 'IT Act s.43A carries a compensation figure of twenty-five thousand rupees' — FALSE, and the prior report was right to refuse the snippet. s.43A contains no cap at all. Rs 25,000 is the s.45 residuary penalty for contravening rules with no separate penalty. Rs 5 crore is the s.46(1A) adjudicating-officer jurisdictional ceiling, a forum rule and not a liability cap.
